User-level testing

Randomize people, not places.

A user-level test randomly splits individual identities into people who can see your ads and people who cannot. This page covers the main designs, what they estimate, the data they need, why they are so noisy, and where they go wrong.

Unit: account, device, cookie, hashed emailIdentity: requiredReads: tracked conversions

Basics

What a user-level test is.

A randomized controlled trial where the unit is one identity. Lift is the outcome in the test group minus the outcome in the holdout, measured on the same population over the same period.

The randomization unit is an individual identity: a logged-in account, a device ID, a cookie, or a hashed email or phone number. Each identity is randomly assigned to test (eligible to see the ads) or control, also called the holdout (not eligible).

Why randomize at all?

A naive comparison of exposed and unexposed people equals the effect on the treated plus selection bias. Random assignment makes potential outcomes independent of treatment, so the selection term drops out1. In advertising that term is large: ads target and reach people who were already more likely to convert, and people who are simply online more.

The author's notes on activity bias and observational vs experimental methods walk through the evidence in more detail.

Activity bias, the canonical warning

In Yahoo! experiments by Lewis, Rao & Reiley (2011), the randomized effect on brand searches was +5.4%. Observational estimates on the same campaign ranged from +1198% with no controls to +872% with day, session, page-view and minutes controls23.

Exposed users were just more active that day. Adding controls barely reduced the bias, and more data would not have helped either: "this is a bias problem, not a variance problem"3.

  • +5.4% Randomized lift in brand searches, against observational estimates of +872% to +1198% on the same Yahoo! campaign Lewis, Rao & Reiley (2011), via the author's notes2
  • 15 US Facebook conversion-lift experiments (about 500M user-experiment observations) where observational methods "often fail" to reproduce the RCT lift, even with rich covariates Gordon et al. (2019)45
  • 663 Facebook experiments where double/debiased ML and stratified propensity matching did not reliably recover the experimental lift Gordon, Moakler & Zettelmeyer (2023)6

How far off were the observational methods?

In the 663-experiment study, the median absolute difference between the RCT lift and the double/debiased ML lift was 115, 103 and 57 percentage points for upper-, mid- and lower-funnel outcomes, against median RCT lifts of only 29%, 18% and 5%. Stratified propensity matching did worse6. arXiv version figures These come from the arXiv preprint; check the published Marketing Science version before quoting them.

The takeaway: if you can randomize at the user level, your estimate is unbiased by design. What remains are three weaknesses: noise, identity coverage, and who runs the measurement. The rest of this page is mostly about those three.

Design types

Five ways to build a holdout.

Every user-level design randomizes identities. They differ in what the control group experiences, and that decides what you can estimate and what it costs.

User-level test designs at a glance
DesignHow control is formedWhat you can estimateMain catch
Intent-to-treat (ITT) holdout A random share of the targetable audience is withheld. Control users never enter the auction for this campaign. The ITT effect: the effect of being eligible for ads. Many test users never see an ad, so the effect is diluted (see ITT vs TOT).
PSA / placebo ads Control users see an unrelated ad, such as a charity PSA, in the same slot. Exposed vs exposed comparison; approximates the effect on the treated (TOT). You pay for placebo impressions, and delivery optimization serves the PSA and the brand ad to different people. For a retargeting campaign a PSA test would be "severely biased"7.
Ghost ads / predicted ghost ads The platform logs when a control user would have been served the ad (won the auction counterfactually) and serves the next-best ad instead7. TOT among would-be-exposed users, with no placebo cost. Needs platform-level integration. Accuracy depends on how well the platform predicts "would have been exposed".
Platform conversion lift (Meta, Google) The platform randomizes accounts or users into test and holdout cells inside its own system89. Incremental conversions, lift % and iROAS for that platform's campaigns. The platform grades its own homework, and coverage is limited to its logged-in users (see pitfalls).
Owned-channel holdouts (email, CRM, push, app) The advertiser randomizes its own customer file. Clean ITT and often TOT, with first-party outcomes. Covers only known customers, so it cannot be used for prospecting media.

As documented

Meta Conversion Lift

Meta "randomizes the audience for your ads and assigns Accounts Center accounts to either the test or control group"8.

A study has one or more cells, each with a treatment and a control (holdout) percentage that sum to 100. Multi-cell studies compare approaches. Start time and treatment percentage lock once the study starts. Access is "currently limited" and goes through a Meta representative8.

As documented

Google Ads Conversion Lift

Two types: based on users, and based on geography910.

Users: test groups built from aggregated user attributes, self-serve for Video, Discovery and Demand Gen; other campaign types go through an account rep. Geography: a geo split that supports offline data, reports incremental ROAS, conversions, value and cost, and "typically requires a higher budget"10. It is a bridge to geo testing.

Two things to double-check

Holdout sizes of "10–20%" for Meta lift studies appear only in third-party vendor blogs, not in Meta's documentation. not an official recommendation Google's availability by campaign type changes over time, so check the current Help pages910 before planning around them.

Estimands

ITT, TOT and dilution.

Only a fraction of the test group is ever exposed: some auctions are lost, frequency caps bind, some users never log in. That gap between "eligible" and "exposed" is the single biggest drag on user-level power.

ITT
Intent-to-treat: the effect of being assigned to the test group, whether or not the user saw an ad. Protected by randomization.
TOT
Treatment-on-the-treated: the effect among users who were actually exposed.
Exposure rate
The share of the test group that saw at least one ad.

The formula

Under one-sided noncompliance, meaning control users cannot be exposed:

ITT = TOT × exposure rate, so TOT = ITT ÷ exposure rate.

Both effects are measured as absolute differences per user (for example, incremental conversions per user). If 20% of the test group sees the ad, the ITT effect is one fifth of the TOT effect. The variance of the outcome, though, still comes from the whole population. Signal falls with the exposure rate while noise stays put, which is why dilution kills power.

How to report it

  • Report ITT as the primary, randomization-protected estimate.
  • Report TOT as ITT divided by the exposure rate, an IV (Wald) estimator.
  • Always report the exposure rate next to the lift.

Never do this

Do not compute TOT by comparing exposed test users with all control users. Exposure is not random within the test group, so that comparison brings selection bias straight back.

Ghost ads were built for this

By identifying would-be-exposed users in control, ghost ads avoid ITT dilution. Johnson, Lewis & Nubbemeyer (2017) report that, compared with PSA and ITT tests, advertisers can measure lift "just as precisely while spending at least an order of magnitude less". In their online retailer's retargeting application the ads lifted site visits by 17.2% and sales by 10.8% (the abstract rounds these to 17% and 11%)7. NBER conference version figures

Fit

When to use it, and when not to.

User-level tests shine when exposure and outcomes can both be tied to the same identity. When either link breaks, reach for a geo test.

Good fit

  • Digital channels with per-identity control: social, programmatic display and video with ghost-ad support, CTV with logged-in IDs, email, CRM and app push.
  • Outcomes joinable to the same identity: online conversions via pixel or CAPI, app events, or hashed CRM and offline matches.
  • Creative, audience, bidding or frequency questions inside one platform. Multi-cell designs answer "which variant drives more incremental conversions", which geo tests rarely have the power for.
  • Results in weeks with large reachable audiences (millions of users).
  • High-volume conversion KPIs that many users trigger, so the test collects enough events to read a small lift. practitioner heuristic

Poor fit

  • Channels without addressable identity: linear TV, radio, out-of-home, print, most brand-search effects, and offline retail sales that cannot be tied to IDs.
  • Cross-channel or total-media questions, such as the total incremental value of the marketing mix.
  • You need an independent read and the measuring party is also the seller.
  • Poor identity coverage, such as heavy iOS opt-out or a logged-out audience, which leaves test and control contaminated or unrepresentative.
  • Small advertisers with small audiences or low conversion volumes (see noise and power).

The core economic problem

Unbiased, but very noisy.

Lewis & Rao (2015) studied 25 large field experiments at major US retailers and brokerages, about $2.8M of digital ad spend. Their conclusion: individual sales are so volatile that even huge experiments return wide intervals11.

  • ≈10 A common coefficient of variation (standard deviation ÷ mean) of individual-level sales, which are very volatile relative to the per-capita cost of the advertising Lewis & Rao (2015)11
  • >10M Person-weeks an informative experiment can require Lewis & Rao (2015)11
  • >100 pp Median width of the confidence interval on ROI across the 25 experiments Lewis & Rao (2015)11

Lewis & Rao still argue RCTs are progress, because they add unbiased information while selection bias "is a crippling concern" for observational methods11. Johnson's practitioner guide makes the same point: display-ad effects are tiny and need large-scale experiments. It also covers power, compliance, identity fragmentation and spillovers12.

What to do about it

  1. Read a "non-significant" result as underpowered, not as proof of zero effect. Report the interval, not the p-value.
  2. Plan for small lifts. The Yahoo! RCT found +5.4%2, and the median lower-funnel lift across 663 Facebook experiments was 5%6. Set the minimum detectable effect (MDE) from break-even economics and prior studies, not from hope.
  3. Buy back signal: reduce variance (CUPED), raise the exposed share (ghost ads instead of diluted ITT), or pick a higher-signal KPI.

Sizing the test

For two means, the sample per group is13:

N per group = 2 (z1−α/2 + z1−β)² σ² / d²

where σ² is the outcome variance and d the MDE. With α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power, (1.96 + 0.84)² ≈ 7.84. Inflate for attrition by dividing by (1 − q)13.

  • Power the ITT effect: expected TOT lift × expected exposure rate.
  • Mind unequal splits. Variance scales with 1/ntest + 1/ncontrol, so a 90/10 split needs a much larger total N than 50/50 for the same MDE.
  • Derive the MDE from break-even economics: usually the lift that makes iROAS equal 1, or your target, at the planned spend.

Significant results from small tests are exaggerated

In underpowered studies, the estimates that clear significance are systematically too large, and some have the wrong sign (Type M and Type S errors)14. The planning page shows how to run design calculations before launch.

Data requirements

What you need before you launch.

The defining requirement is one stable identity that the media platform uses for delivery and that outcomes can be joined to.

Data requirements for user-level tests
DimensionRequirement
Identity / unitA logged-in account, consented MAID/IDFA, first-party cookie, or hashed email or phone. Cross-device users need a person-level graph, or test and control will leak (see pitfalls).
GranularityUser-level (or platform cell-level) exposure logs, the assignment flag, and timestamped conversion events. Ideally impression-level logs to compute exposure rate and frequency.
Number of unitsTypically hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of users per arm, following Lewis & Rao's CV ≈ 10 and >10M person-weeks finding11. Compute it from your own baseline rate and variance.
KPIsConversions from pixel, CAPI or SDK, or matched offline (CRM, POS). Pick one primary KPI in advance. Purchase revenue is noisier than conversion counts, so consider winsorizing or capping it.
Pre-period historyNot needed for identification, but pre-period outcomes for the same users are what make CUPED work. Typically 1–4 weeks per user. rule of thumb, unverified
Conversion windowLong enough for the purchase cycle plus lagged effects. Fix it before the test starts.
AccessPlatform lift studies return aggregates only. Independent analysis needs a clean room, such as Google Ads Data Hub15, or a partner that can join exposure and outcomes under privacy constraints.

Identity is eroding.

Privacy changes shrink the slice of users you can randomize and match, and that slice is not random.

iOS 14.5 and later

Apple ATT

Apps must ask permission through the AppTrackingTransparency framework "to track them or access their device's advertising identifier"16. Opted-out users drop out of cross-app matching, so assignment and attribution cover a smaller, non-random group, and results may not generalize.

As of April 22, 2025

Third-party cookies

Google reversed its planned Chrome deprecation: it keeps the current user-choice approach and "will not be rolling out a new standalone prompt for third-party cookies"17. Safari and Firefox are widely reported to block them by default. unverified against browser vendor docs Prefer logged-in or first-party IDs over cookies.

Independent reads

Clean rooms

Clean rooms such as Google Ads Data Hub15 join first-party outcomes to platform exposure logs and return aggregated, privacy-thresholded results. You gain independent outcome data and custom analysis. You pay with thresholds that block small-segment reads, engineering setup, and analysis bound to one platform's IDs.

Dated, and still moving

Privacy policies change. The ATT and Chrome positions above reflect the cited pages at the time this guide's research was compiled; check them again before you rely on them. The broad implication holds either way: user-level tests increasingly run inside walled gardens or clean rooms, while geo tests need no identity at all.

Variance reduction

CUPED: get more out of the same users.

Use what you already know about each user before the test to strip predictable noise out of the result.

CUPED (Deng, Xu, Kohavi & Walker, WSDM 2013) uses pre-experiment data on the same users as a covariate to reduce metric variance and so improve sensitivity18. It is close in spirit to ANCOVA or regression adjustment; the author's note on the logic of regression adjustment explains the reasoning19.

  • Use pre-period values only. The covariate must be unaffected by treatment18.
  • It buys precision, not unbiasedness. Under randomization the estimate is already unbiased; adjustment narrows the interval.
  • Gains track the pre/post correlation. With the optimal adjustment, the variance of the estimate is multiplied by (1 − ρ²), where ρ is the correlation between pre- and post-period outcomes: the reduction is the squared correlation.
  • Best for repeat-purchase KPIs. Gains are smallest for first-purchase or acquisition KPIs, where most users have no pre-period activity.

No variance-reduction figure quoted here

The squared-correlation relationship is standard CUPED algebra, not a figure from the CUPED paper. This guide quotes no typical percentage reduction, because none was verified. Estimate yours from your own pre-period data.

Threats

Pitfalls that bias or bury the result.

Randomization protects you from selection bias. It does not protect you from leaky identities, spillovers, self-grading platforms or impatient analysts.

ITT dilution

A low exposure rate shrinks the measurable effect linearly while leaving the noise unchanged. Always report the exposure rate alongside the lift, and power the test on the diluted ITT effect (see ITT vs TOT).

Cross-device and identity contamination

A person randomized to control on one ID may be in test on another: phone vs laptop, logged-in vs cookie. Their outcomes then land in both arms, biasing lift toward zero.

Use person-level (logged-in) IDs, assign by household or person where you can, and treat cookie-level results as lower bounds. direction assumes contamination only adds exposure to control

Spillovers and interference

Ads can reach untreated users indirectly through word of mouth, shared household devices, or retargeting pools seeded by test-group behavior. That violates SUTVA, the assumption that one unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome. Johnson's guide flags spillovers as a modern challenge12. When spillovers matter, geo randomization is the usual remedy. Within a user-level test, exclude control users from retargeting pools, freeze other campaigns' targeting changes during the test, and coordinate with other teams testing on the same audience.

Walled gardens grading their own homework

Platform lift studies are true RCTs, but the platform designs the study, defines the conversions it can see, and reports the result.

  • Conversion coverage is limited to what the pixel, CAPI or SDK reports; offline sales and other channels are missing.
  • Methodology is opaque and changes over time.
  • Each platform measures only itself, so platform reads cannot be summed into a cross-channel total.

Mitigate: send your own first-party conversions (CAPI or offline uploads), replicate in a clean room, and periodically calibrate platform lift against geo tests. Google's geography-based lift is one design that takes outcomes outside the user graph910.

Delivery-optimization bias

PSA designs and "A/B test" tools that split campaigns rather than users let the delivery algorithm send each arm to different people. You end up comparing audiences, not ads. Only user-level randomization before the auction gives a causal estimate; this is the core argument against PSA designs7.

Sample ratio mismatch and logging bugs

Check that the realized test/control split matches the design (for example a chi-square test on counts) and that pre-period covariates are balanced; the STAR example in the author's notes shows balance checks1. A mismatch usually signals an assignment or logging bug, not a treatment effect.

Peeking, novelty and short windows

Stopping when the dashboard turns significant inflates false positives. Short tests miss lagged conversions and over-weight novelty effects. Fix the duration and analysis date in advance, and pre-register the KPI, estimand, segments and decision rule: data-contingent choices inflate false positives even without deliberate fishing2021.

Many cells, KPIs, segments and readout dates

Multiplicity hides in multi-cell designs, long KPI lists, and segment cuts. With 10 independent tests at α = 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive is about 40%; with 100 it is about 99%. Holm, Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg corrections help22; the planning page covers them and the Bayesian alternative.

Summary

Pros and cons.

Pros

  • Unbiased by design when randomization is respected14.
  • Fine-grained: multi-cell designs compare creative, audiences, frequency and bidding8.
  • Fast to set up on major platforms, with no need for long geo histories.
  • CUPED and ghost ads can sharply improve precision per dollar718.
  • Results can be segmented by audience attributes9.

Cons

  • Requires addressable identity, which ATT and browser cookie blocking are eroding1617.
  • Noisy: small true effects need huge samples, and wide ROI intervals are the norm11.
  • ITT dilution and cross-device contamination bias lift toward zero.
  • Walled gardens measure themselves, with limited outcome coverage and opaque methods.
  • Cannot measure offline-only channels or total cross-channel effects, and spillovers violate SUTVA.
