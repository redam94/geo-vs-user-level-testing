Test planning

Plan the test before you buy the media.

How to take a media incrementality test from question to readout: the KPI, power and MDE, design, markets or audiences, timing, budget, contamination controls, launch checks, analysis plan, and how the result feeds your MMM.

Phases: pre-test, in-flight, postApplies to: geo and user-level testsOutput: a pre-registered plan

The plan on one page

Twelve steps, three phases.

Most of the work happens before launch. Once media is live, your job is to keep the test healthy, not to read results. After the flight, you analyze exactly what you wrote down.

Step 1

Start with the decision, not the dashboard.

A test exists to change a decision. Write the decision down first, then pick the one number that would change it.

State the decision the test feeds, for example "cut or keep linear TV in region X", and the estimand: iROAS, lift % or incremental conversions. Decide one-sided or two-sided in advance. A one-sided test gains power when the direction is known1.

Pick the business outcome closest to the decision, such as sales or new customers. Proxy KPIs raise power but must be validated, and leading indicators help when the post-period is short2. For user-level tests, choose one primary KPI in advance. Purchase revenue is noisier than a conversion count, so consider winsorizing or capping it.

In user-level tests, many people in the test group never see an ad. Decide whether you report the effect of being eligible (ITT) or of being exposed (TOT). Under one-sided noncompliance, ITT = TOT × exposure rate: if 20% of the test group sees the ad, the ITT effect is one fifth of the TOT effect, while the noise still comes from the whole population3.

iROAS
Incremental revenue divided by the spend that caused it. In geo-based regression it is the coefficient on incremental spend4.
Lift %
Incremental outcome as a share of the counterfactual outcome.
Incremental conversions
Test-group outcome minus the counterfactual, in units.
ITT
Intent-to-treat: the effect of being eligible for ads. Protected by randomization; report it as the primary estimate.
TOT
Treatment-on-the-treated: ITT divided by the exposure rate (a Wald estimator).
MDE
Minimum detectable effect: the smallest effect the design can reliably detect at the chosen α and power5.

Tip: derive the MDE from break-even economics

The smallest lift worth detecting is usually the one that makes iROAS equal to 1, or to your target, given the planned spend. A test that can only detect lifts far above that line cannot answer the business question.

Step 2

Write it down before the data arrives.

Data-contingent analysis choices inflate false positives even without deliberate fishing. A timestamped plan removes the forks67.

Gelman and Loken's "garden of forking paths" is the reason to pre-register: every choice made after seeing the data (which KPI, which window, which segments, which outliers) is another chance to find a result that is not there6. An internal document, timestamped before launch, is enough.

Geo designs are pre-registrable too. Synthetic-control weights depend only on pre-period data, so the market sets, donor pool and weights can be locked before any post-period outcome exists. That guards against specification searching2.

Note

Pre-registration does not forbid exploratory analysis. It labels it. Segments you did not list as confirmatory are hypotheses for the next test, not findings from this one.

Step 3

Power, MDE and sample size.

Advertising effects are small and outcomes are noisy. Power is the constraint that decides whether a test is worth running at all.

  • ≈10Typical coefficient of variation of individual sales in large ad experimentsLewis & Rao (2015)8
  • >10MPerson-weeks an informative experiment can easily requireLewis & Rao (2015)8
  • >100 ppMedian ROI confidence-interval width across 25 experiments (about $2.8M of spend)Lewis & Rao (2015)8

User-level tests: closed-form power

The inputs are α (often 0.05, two-sided), power 1−β (0.80 or 0.90), the baseline variance σ², and the MDE you care about economically5.

Two means, N per group:

N = 2 (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² σ² / d²

With α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, (1.96 + 0.84)² ≈ 7.845.

Rearranged for a sample you can actually afford, the same formula gives the MDE: d = (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β) · σ · √(1/ntest + 1/ncontrol), which is about 2.8 · σ · √(2/N) for a 50/50 split at α = 0.05 and 80% power. This is the algebraic inverse of the formula above, not a separate result.

Two proportions (conversion rate), with allocation ratio r:

N = (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² · p̄(1−p̄)(1+r) / (r·d²)

Here p̄ is the average conversion rate, d the difference in rates and r = n₁/n₂5. Solved this way, N is the size of group 2; group 1 gets r·N.

Attrition: inflate to N / (1−q). Base the effect size on prior studies or pilots, not hope5.

Three ad-specific adjustments

  • Power the ITT, not the TOT. Expected ITT effect = expected TOT lift × expected exposure rate. Dilution shrinks the signal but not the noise3.
  • Unequal splits cost sample. Variance scales with 1/ntest + 1/ncontrol, so a 90/10 split needs a much larger total N than 50/50 for the same MDE: 1/0.9 + 1/0.1 ≈ 11.1 against 4 for 50/50, or about 2.8 times the total sample.
  • Start from break-even. Use the lift that makes iROAS = 1 (or your target) as the MDE.

Geo tests: power by simulation

Geo tests are almost always powered by simulation on historical data, not by closed-form formulas.

  • GeoLift's GeoLiftMarketSelection() simulates synthetic effects over candidate market sets, durations and effect sizes. The walkthrough uses effect sizes of 0–50% in 5-point steps, α = 0.1, 2–5 test markets and 10–15 day tests, and accepts a budget and cost per incremental conversion (cpic). Re-check the top candidates with a longer lookback window to confirm that power holds over time9.
  • Google recommends stating precision as a target CI half-width on iROAS (for example ±1.0) together with a false-positive rate4.
  • A bigger spend contrast buys more power, limited by inventory and diminishing returns4.
  • The expected effect must be large relative to geo-level noise. A small effect in a short test is the case to avoid2.

Levers that buy power

  • Ghost ads instead of PSA or plain ITT holdouts: advertisers can measure lift "just as precisely while spending at least an order of magnitude less"3.
  • CUPED or regression adjustment on pre-period outcomes of the same users. The covariate must be unaffected by treatment1011. Residual variance is about (1 − ρ²) times the original, where ρ is the pre/post correlation, so gains are largest for repeat-purchase KPIs and smallest for acquisition KPIs.
  • A higher-signal KPI, validated against the business outcome2.
  • A one-sided test, when the direction is known in advance1.

Caveat: plan for small lifts

Expect true lifts in the low single digits for many upper-funnel campaigns, and do not plan around optimistic 20–30% lifts practitioner heuristic. The CUPED "squared correlation" rule is standard algebra, not a figure from the CUPED paper; no specific variance-reduction magnitudes are quoted here because none were verified.

Beyond power

Wrong signs, inflated effects, too many looks.

Power tells you how often you will detect an effect. It does not tell you how wrong a "significant" estimate can be, or how many false positives a stack of KPIs and segments will produce.

Type S and Type M errors

Underpowered tests that clear significance are systematically exaggerated.

Type S: a significant estimate has the wrong sign. Type M: it exaggerates the true magnitude (exaggeration ratio = |estimate| / |truth|). Large estimates are a by-product of large standard errors12. Gelman & Carlin recommend design calculations from realistic external effect sizes: compute the probability of a wrong sign and the expected exaggeration, not power alone13.

In practice: assume a plausible true lift (for example 2–3%), compute your design's standard error, and report P(Type S) and the expected exaggeration ratio. If the ratio is above 2, redesign before launch rule of thumb.

Multiple testing

With m independent tests at α = 0.05, P(at least one false positive) = 1 − 0.95m.

In lift studies, multiplicity hides in multi-cell designs, many KPIs, many segments (age × gender × region) and several readout dates. Bonferroni and Holm control the family-wise error rate (conservative); Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate (more power)14.

The Bayesian alternative is partial pooling: hierarchical models shrink noisy subgroup estimates, reducing Type S and M errors by improving the estimates rather than widening intervals1215.

Caveat: bias is not Type M

The author's notes frame the Yahoo! gap (observational +1198% vs randomized +5.4%) as an extreme, roughly 220×, Type-M-style exaggeration12. Strictly, that gap is bias from violated identification, not sampling-driven Type M error. Treat it as an illustration, not a definition.

Step 4

Choose the design that fits the question.

The channel, the KPI and the number of available units decide most of this. For the full side-by-side, see Compare.

  1. Is the channel impossible to target person by person (TV, radio, OOH, print), or is the KPI only observed offline or in aggregate?

    Geo test. It needs no user identity and captures offline sales and delayed conversions4. See Geo testing.

  2. About 30 or more geos, and you can randomize?

    Stratified randomized geo experiment with geo-based regression, or a paired design with Trimmed Match when geos are heterogeneous416.

  3. Few geos, or a single market?

    Time-based regression or synthetic control, with an algorithmic matched-market design. TBR works down to one test region against one control1718.

  4. Several treated geos and imperfect pre-period fit?

    Augmented synthetic control (GeoLift) or generalized synthetic control. GSC needs a large control pool and a long pre-period919.

  5. An always-on channel where you want to limit the revenue at risk?

    Go-dark holdout (Inverse GeoLift). GeoLift says "ongoing media is best tested with an Inverse GeoLift"20.

  6. A phased rollout across markets?

    Staggered difference-in-differences (Callaway–Sant'Anna), not a plain two-way fixed-effects regression, which can put negative weights on some effects2122.

  7. A digital channel with addressable identity, and a question about creative, audience, bidding or frequency?

    User-level multi-cell test, such as a platform conversion-lift study with several cells23. See User-level testing.

  8. The platform offers ghost ads?

    Prefer ghost ads to PSA designs. Delivery optimization serves PSA and brand ads to different people; Johnson et al. show a PSA test would be "severely biased" for a retargeting campaign3.

  9. You need a read on a walled garden that does not come from the walled garden?

    Send your own conversions, replicate in a clean room, and calibrate platform lift against geo tests. Google's geography-based Conversion Lift takes outcomes outside the user graph and supports offline data24.

Step 5

Pick the units: markets or audiences.

For geo tests the hard part is finding comparable, uncontaminated markets. For user tests it is finding an identity that both the ad platform and your outcome data can see.

Geo markets

  • Randomize when possible. Stratify by geo size, and possibly by region4. Use paired designs when geos are heterogeneous16.
  • Matched markets when you cannot randomize: let an algorithm search (TBR matched markets, GeoLift market selection) rather than intuition189.
  • Enough units. Geo-based regression prefers 30 or more geos; GeoLift asks for at least 20 geo-units425. Permutation inference is not meaningful with fewer than about 5 donors2.
  • Avoid dominant markets. London and Paris "dominate entire countries"4. Treated units should sit inside the donors' convex hull2.
  • Exclude contaminated donors: neighbours of treated geos and geos running similar interventions2.
  • Clean data. Location, date and KPI with no missing location-dates; GeoLift drops locations with gaps9.

User audiences

  • One ID for delivery and outcomes. A logged-in account, consented device ID, first-party cookie or hashed email that the platform delivers to and that conversions can be joined to.
  • Person over cookie. Cross-device users need a person-level ID, or test and control leak into each other.
  • Randomize before the auction. Splitting campaigns instead of users lets the delivery algorithm send each arm to different people3. Meta's lift studies randomize Accounts Center accounts23.
  • Check identity coverage. iOS users who opt out under App Tracking Transparency drop out of cross-app matching, leaving a smaller, non-random slice26.
  • Size the audience. Typically hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of users per arm, following from the Lewis & Rao noise levels8. Compute it from your own baseline rate and variance.

Caveat: lag guidance is thin

GeoLift has a blog post on lagged effects, but its specific recommendations were not verified for this guide. A ghost-ads "funnel and carry-over" meta-study exists, but its numbers were not verified either. Size the cooldown from your own purchase-cycle data.

Step 6, continued

What the test costs.

A test has two bills: the media needed to move the KPI past the MDE, and the revenue you give up in the holdout. Compute both before launch, and feed the budget into the power simulation as a design input9.

Spend to reach the MDE

In a heavy-up, the incremental spend in treated geos must be large enough to reach the MDE. In a go-dark, the treated geos need enough current spend to withhold20. For user tests, expected incremental conversions must exceed the MDE, which Lewis & Rao show can be prohibitive for low-margin, high-variance businesses8.

Cost of the holdout

Go-dark tests forgo revenue, and standard GeoLifts often need more than half of locations in control, which is why inverse designs exist20. In user tests the cost is the unexposed control audience; unequal splits cut it but need more total sample.

Cost of the design

PSA designs pay for placebo impressions; ghost ads avoid that and cut experimentation cost3. Google's geography-based Conversion Lift "typically requires a higher budget" than the user-based type24.

In GeoLift, pass the budget and cost per incremental conversion (cpic) into the market-selection simulation to impose a budget constraint on the candidate designs9. Google's matched-markets search likewise restricts candidate designs to those that meet budget and geo-assignment constraints18.

Budget reality check

Lewis & Rao's 25 experiments spent about $2.8M in total and still produced a median ROI interval more than 100 percentage points wide8. If the affordable design cannot reach the break-even MDE, change the design (bigger contrast, ghost ads, a better KPI) or do not run the test.

Step 7

Keep the control group clean.

When leakage adds exposure to the control group, or spillover benefits it, the estimate is pulled toward zero, so a leaky test tends to understate the effect.

Geo tests

  • Use commuting zones or other coarser units, which GeoLift supports to "take the commuter effect into account"28, or drop buffer geos along treated borders from the donor pool2.
  • Freeze other media, pricing and promotions in test and control, or log them as covariates25.
  • Measure total business, not just the channel's own KPI: when eBay turned off brand-keyword paid search in 68 DMAs for 60 days, almost all of the lost traffic moved to organic search29.
  • Digital geo-targeting relies on IP location, which is noisy practitioner heuristic

User-level tests

  • Assign at the person or household level where possible; treat cookie-level results as lower bounds.
  • Exclude control users from retargeting pools seeded by test-group behaviour.
  • Freeze other campaigns' targeting changes and coordinate with other teams testing on the same audience.
  • When word of mouth or shared devices make spillovers likely, SUTVA is violated; geo randomization is the usual remedy.

Why contamination gives a lower bound

When spillover cannot be removed, synthetic-control estimates are biased toward zero; if the treatment benefits donors, the estimate is a lower bound on the effect2. Cross-device leakage in user tests biases lift toward zero in the same way, under the usual assumption that contamination only adds exposure to the control group.

Caveat

Border-spillover mechanisms (TV and radio signals crossing DMA lines, IP geolocation error, shipping addresses that differ from where the ad was seen) are practitioner knowledge, not drawn from a cited source. The link between commuting zones and spillover is an inference from GeoLift's commuter-effect documentation.

In-flight

Launch QA, then hands off.

An assignment or logging bug can invalidate a test before any statistics run. Check the plumbing in the first days, then stop looking at the result until the analysis date.

Launch checks

  • Sample ratio mismatchA chi-square test on realized test/control counts against the design. A mismatch usually signals an assignment or logging bug, not a treatment effect.
  • Covariate balanceCompare pre-period outcomes and covariates across arms30.
  • Control really unexposedControl users never enter the auction; in Inverse GeoLift, control geos with positive weights actually receive the media20.
  • Exposure logging worksYou will need the exposure rate to interpret ITT.
  • Spend lands where plannedPlanned and actual spend per geo per period, needed for iROAS4.
  • Concurrent-activity log startedOther campaigns, local media, pricing and distribution changes per geo25.

Monitor health, not lift

During the flight, watch pacing, delivery, the sample ratio and data feeds. Do not watch the lift estimate with a decision in mind.

Pitfall: peeking

Stopping when the dashboard turns significant inflates false positives, and short tests miss lagged conversions and over-weight novelty effects. Fix the duration and the analysis date in advance.

Some platforms enforce this for you: Meta locks a lift study's start time and treatment percentage once it starts23.

Diagnostics to pre-specify

Pre-period fit

Plot the synthetic series against the treated series and report pre-period RMSPE1. GeoLift reports a scaled L2 imbalance from 0 (perfect fit) to 1 (no better than a simple average of controls)9.

Backdating (in-time placebo)

Pretend the test started earlier. A credible model shows roughly zero "effect" in the backdated window1.

Leave-one-out

Drop each donor or geo in turn. The estimate should not depend on a single geo14.

Placebo geos

Run the analysis on untreated geos and expect a null. In the CausalImpact paper's geo experiment, the placebo on untreated DMAs gave 2% [−6%, 10%]27.

Balance and sample ratio (user tests)

Repeat the launch checks on the final data, and report the exposure rate next to the lift30.

Do

  • Report the interval, not just the p-value; a non-significant result is usually underpowered, not proof of zero8.
  • Report ITT as primary and TOT as ITT ÷ exposure rate.
  • Label unplanned segments as exploratory.

Don't

  • Compute TOT by comparing exposed test users with all control users: that reintroduces selection.
  • Use plain two-way fixed effects on a staggered rollout22.
  • Prefer an estimator because its interval is narrower. In the CausalImpact geo example, its 95% interval (13–30%) was wider than classical regression's (19–22%) with point estimates within 5%27.

Caveat: check aggregation choices

A third-party replication reported that GeoLift's conformal p-value changes when treated outcomes are summed rather than averaged (p ≈ 0.68 vs ≈ 0.01) unverified. It has not been checked against Meta's own documentation; pre-register the aggregation. GeoLift's default use of conformal inference also comes from secondary documentation pages.

Post

Read out, then feed the model.

A test is one unbiased, local measurement of one channel. That is often exactly the information a media mix model lacks, so feed it back into the model that allocates the whole budget31. See Other methods.

What the readout contains

  • The point estimate and interval for the pre-registered KPI and estimand.
  • iROAS, and for user tests the exposure rate.
  • The decision rule and whether it was met.
  • Diagnostics, deviations from the plan, and exploratory findings labelled as such.
  • Scope: the population, period and spend level the result applies to. Geo results are average effects for the treated geos; carrying them to other markets or spend levels is an assumption.

Three ways to calibrate an MMM

  • As priors (Meridian). An experiment's ROI estimate and standard error become an ROI prior; CalibrationBuilder adjusts for spend, recency and duration3132.
  • As an extra likelihood (PyMC-Marketing). Each test is an extra observation of the saturation curve: f(x + Δx) − f(x) must match the measured lift within its standard error33.
  • As a selection objective (Robyn). MAPE.LIFT penalizes models whose decomposed contribution over the test window differs from the experiment34.

Match the estimand

MMM ROI uses a zero-spend counterfactual. Many experiments measure marginal lift or lift against business-as-usual, and translating between them adds uncertainty beyond the experiment's standard error. Check each test's recency, duration, granularity, population and complexity31. A platform conversion-lift test measures that platform's audience and window, not total-business offline sales practitioner heuristic. Calibration is also more complex for inverse (go-dark) designs20.

Choose the next test with the model

Prioritize channels with large spend, wide MMM posteriors or implausible ROI; Meridian's channel-calibration score flags them35. Run tests at the spend levels the MMM will extrapolate to, and refresh them as effects drift practitioner heuristic

  1. MMM flags a channel

    High spend, wide posterior or implausible ROI35.
