Geo-level testing

Randomize regions, not people.

How geo experiments work, which design and estimator to pick, what data they need, how to check the fit, and where they quietly go wrong.

Unit: DMA, state, commuting zoneIdentity: noneReads: total business, offline

Basics

What a geo test is.

Change advertising in some places, leave it alone in others, and compare what each place sold. The geo, not the person, is the unit of assignment and analysis.

A geo-level test changes advertising in some geographic units (DMAs, states, commuting zones, postal codes, cities) and leaves it unchanged in others. It then compares an aggregate KPI per geo, such as sales, sign-ups, store visits or site sessions, against a counterfactual: what the treated geos would have done without the change.

Google's 2011 paper made the case for advertisers. Non-overlapping regions are randomly assigned to control or treatment, and the paper argues that geo experiments are "conceptually simple" and "provide results that are easy to interpret"1.

The main reason to work at the geo level is that it needs no user identity. User-level randomization online suffers from multiple devices and cookie churn. Geo experiments sidestep both and "capture the full effects of advertising, including offline sales and conversions over longer periods of time"2.

Everything else on this page follows from one fact: you have few units. Even in the US, Google's national geo experiments work with "over 210 geos"2, and most countries have far fewer regions. That is why the choice of design and estimator matters so much more here than in a user-level test.

Geo
The unit you randomize and analyze: a DMA, state, commuting zone, postal code or city.
Counterfactual
The KPI the treated geos would have produced without the media change. Every estimator below is a different way to build it.
Donor pool
The untreated geos available to build that counterfactual.
iROAS
Incremental return on ad spend: incremental revenue divided by the change in spend2.
Pre-period
History before the test, used to learn how treated and control geos move together.

Design families

Six ways to split the map.

The design decides which geos get the change and what the change is. Pick it from how many geos you have and whether the channel is new or already running.

Many geos

Randomized geo experiment (GBR)

Many geos are randomly split, often stratified by size.

Analyzed with geo-based regression. Suits national channels in large countries where many geos are available12.

Few geos

Matched markets (TBR)

A few hand-picked or algorithmically matched test and control markets.

For when few geos are available or the campaign is limited to some markets. Analyzed with time-based regression, down to one market against one34.

Heterogeneous geos

Matched pairs (Trimmed Match)

Pair geos first, then flip a coin within each pair.

Gives robust iROAS estimates when geos differ a lot in size and behaviour5.

More spend

Heavy-up / scale test

Treated geos get extra spend on top of business as usual.

Measures the marginal return at a higher spend level, not the value of the channel as a whole.

Ongoing channel

Go-dark / holdout (Inverse GeoLift)

Switch media off in the treated geos.

GeoLift's guidance: "ongoing media is best tested with an Inverse GeoLift"6. It holds out only the treatment group, which limits opportunity cost.

Phased launch

Staggered rollout

Geos adopt the treatment at different dates.

Common for phased launches or for measuring a change after the fact. Needs a heterogeneity-robust DiD estimator7.

A famous go-dark

eBay turned off paid search in 68 DMAs for 60 days. Brand-keyword ads had no measurable short-term benefit, because almost all of the lost traffic moved to organic search8.

Estimators

How the counterfactual gets built.

Each estimator answers the same question, "what would the test geos have done?", with a different recipe. Open one for the intuition, then use the table to compare them.

Geo-based regression (GBR)

Intuition: across many geos, geos that got more incremental spend should show more test-period response than their own history predicts. The slope of that relationship is the iROAS.

GBR regresses each geo's test-period response on its pre-period response and its incremental spend. It is weighted by 1/pre-period volume to handle heteroscedasticity, so geos with near-zero volume get very large weights and the smallest geos are best combined. Google prefers it "for experiments with 30, or more, geos" and points to time-series methods when fewer are available2.

Time-based regression (TBR)

Intuition: learn how the test markets track the control markets before the test, then use that relationship to predict what the test markets "should" have done during it.

TBR regresses the aggregated treatment series on the aggregated control series over the pre-period, projects that relationship into the test period, and reads the cumulative causal effect off the gap. It works for few geos, including a single test region against a single control region3. Google's open-source matched_markets library pairs TBR analysis with a greedy matched-markets design search restricted to designs that meet budget and geo-assignment constraints94.

Synthetic control (SC)

Intuition: no single control market looks like your test market, but a blend might. Find the weighted mix of untreated geos whose history traces the test market's history, and let that blend play the role of "the test market without ads".

The counterfactual is a convex, non-negative weighted combination of donor geos, with weights chosen so the blend tracks the treated unit's pre-period KPI1011. As summarized in Synthetic Control Requirements, it has three advantages over regression12:

  • No extrapolation. Weights are non-negative and sum to 1.
  • Transparency. Named donors and sparse weights that a domain expert can sanity-check.
  • Pre-registrable. Weights depend only on pre-period data, so the design can be locked before any outcome is seen.
Augmented, penalized and generalized SC; synthetic DiD

Intuition: plain SC fails when no blend fits well or when several geos are treated. These variants patch those gaps.

  • Augmented SC (ASCM) adds an outcome model, such as ridge regression, to correct bias when the pre-period fit is imperfect, that is, when the treated unit sits outside the donors' convex hull1314. Meta's GeoLift is built on ASCM; its model options are none, ridge, GSC, or "best"15.
  • Penalized SC (Abadie & L'Hour) handles multiple treated units by penalizing donors far from each treated unit, which gives unique, sparse weights14.
  • Generalized SC (GSC) fits an interactive fixed-effects model on control units, projects the treated units' pre-period onto the estimated factors and imputes their counterfactuals. It handles multiple treated units and staggered timing, picks the number of factors by cross-validation and gives parametric-bootstrap standard errors. Ordinary DiD is the special case with constant factor loadings1617. It needs both a large control pool and a long pre-period; a short pre-period makes the loadings imprecise and the estimate biased17.
  • Synthetic DiD combines unit weights, as in SC, with time weights, as in DiD18.
Difference-in-differences (DiD), including staggered rollouts

Intuition: the control geos tell you how much the test geos would have changed anyway. Subtract that from how much they actually changed.

DiD compares the treated group's pre-to-post change with the control group's change. It relies on parallel trends: without treatment, the groups would have moved together19.

Staggered rollouts are where DiD bites. A plain two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) regression can put negative weights on some cohort-period effects, and its coefficient can even have the wrong sign when effects change over time2021. Callaway & Sant'Anna first estimate group-time effects ATT(g,t), then aggregate them into event-study, cohort or overall summaries, using never-treated or not-yet-treated geos as comparisons, with optional covariates and doubly-robust estimators72122. In the paper's minimum-wage application, TWFE found "no effect" while the CS estimator found about −3.1%21.

The framework allows a known anticipation horizon, and the authors warn against choosing between parallel-trends assumptions by pre-testing22.

Bayesian DiD

Intuition: the same DiD comparison, written so you get a full distribution of plausible effects instead of one number and a standard error.

Bayesian DiD writes the model as a Bayesian regression, for example in PyMC, which gives a full posterior for the effect and explicit counterfactual draws. The identifying assumptions do not change: parallel trends still has to hold23.

CausalImpact (Bayesian structural time series) on geos

Intuition: forecast the test geos' KPI from their own trend and seasonality plus the control geos, as if the campaign never ran, and treat the forecast error after launch as the effect.

CausalImpact is a Bayesian state-space model with a local trend, seasonality and a regression on control series. A spike-and-slab prior selects which controls to use, and posterior predictive draws give the counterfactual and credible intervals for pointwise, cumulative and running-average effects2425. DiD and SC are special cases of it25. The paper's own application is a geo experiment, walked through in the worked example below26.

Fit

When a geo test is the right tool.

Geo tests shine where identity is missing or the outcome happens offline. They struggle when the effect is small and the map is small.

Use it when

  • The KPI is only observed in aggregate or offline: retail sales, call centers, dealer sales, or anything without user identity.
  • The channel cannot be targeted or randomized per person: TV, radio, OOH, print, or walled gardens without a lift tool.
  • You need cross-channel or total-business incrementality, including halo and organic substitution8.
  • Cookie or device loss makes user-level holdouts leaky2.
  • You need experimental iROAS to calibrate an MMM. GeoLift notes calibration is more complex for inverse (go-dark) designs6.

Avoid it, or switch designs, when

  • The expected effect is small relative to geo-level noise and the test is short12.
  • Too few comparable, uncontaminated geos exist. Permutation inference is not meaningful with fewer than about 5 donors (vault summary of Abadie 2021)12.
  • The treated market is unlike any combination of donors, such as a dominant capital city122.
  • User-level randomization is available and identity is clean: with many more units it is usually far more powerful per dollar practitioner heuristic

Which geo design and estimator?

  1. About 30 or more geos, and you can randomize?

    Stratified randomized geo experiment with GBR, or a paired design with Trimmed Match25.

  2. Few geos, or a single market?

    TBR or synthetic control, with algorithmic matched-market design39.

  3. Several treated geos and an imperfect pre-period fit?

    Augmented SC (GeoLift) or generalized SC131516.

  4. Ongoing channel, and you want to minimize revenue risk?

    Inverse GeoLift / go-dark holdout6.

  5. Phased rollout across geos?

    Callaway–Sant'Anna staggered DiD7.

  6. Want a full posterior or explicit trend and seasonality modelling?

    CausalImpact / BSTS, or Bayesian DiD2423.

Trade-offs

Pros and cons.

The strengths come from aggregation. So do the weaknesses.

Pros

  • Privacy-durable and identity-free; works on first-party sales data2.
  • Captures offline sales, delayed conversions and cross-channel effects2.
  • Works for media that cannot be targeted to individuals.
  • True randomization is possible with enough geos1, and even matched-market SC designs can be pre-registered12.
  • Results come as iROAS and incremental units, which finance and MMM teams can use.

Cons

  • Few units, so low power. Just over 210 geos in the US2, far fewer regions in most countries, so intervals are wide.
  • Opportunity cost. Go-dark tests forgo revenue, and standard GeoLifts often need more than half of locations in control6.
  • Spillover across borders from commuting, media signals that cross DMA lines and shipping addresses that differ from where the ad was seen practitioner knowledge
  • Imperfect geo-targeting in digital platforms, because IP-based location is noisy practitioner heuristic
  • Local shocks (weather, competitor promotions, store openings, local news) can swamp the signal12.
  • Results are average effects for the treated geos. Carrying them to other markets or spend levels is an assumption.

Data

The minimum is a clean panel of location, date and KPI. Power comes from how many geos you have and how much stable history sits behind them.

The pre-period guidance disagrees

GeoLift asks for a pre-period of 4–5× the test length and ideally a year of history27, while Google's GBR examples use 4 to 8 weeks2. One likely reason is that they describe different estimators: GBR uses the pre-period as one covariate per geo, while synthetic-control methods learn weights from the whole pre-period series this guide's inference Follow the guidance for the estimator you will actually run.

Design

Plan it before you run it.

Geo tests are powered by simulation on your own history, not by a formula. The full checklist lives on the planning page; these are the geo-specific steps.

  1. State the decision and estimand

    Name the decision the test feeds, such as "cut or keep linear TV in region X", and the estimand: iROAS, lift % or incremental conversions. Decide one-sided or two-sided in advance; one-sided tests gain power when the direction is known29.

  2. Simulate power and MDE

    GeoLift's GeoLiftMarketSelection() injects synthetic effects into candidate market sets, durations and effect sizes; its walkthrough uses effects of 0–50% in 5-point steps, α = 0.1, 2–5 test markets and 10–15 day tests, with optional budget and cpic constraints. Re-check the top candidates with a longer lookback window15. Google suggests stating precision as a target CI half-width on iROAS (for example ±1.0) plus a false-positive rate2. A bigger spend contrast means more power, limited by inventory and diminishing returns2.

  3. Select and assign markets

    Randomize when possible, stratified by geo size2, or paired when geos are heterogeneous5. Otherwise use algorithmic matched-market search (TBRMM greedy search, GeoLift market selection) rather than intuition915. Avoid dominant markets: London and Paris "dominate entire countries"2. Exclude neighbours of treated geos and geos running similar interventions from the donor pool12.

  4. Set duration, cooldown and budget

    Run for at least one full purchase cycle27: GeoLift suggests ≥15 days for daily data or 4–6 weeks for weekly data27; Google's GBR examples run 3–5 weeks2. Keep measuring after media stops, because offline conversions "may well be delayed by days or even weeks"2. In a heavy-up the extra spend must be large enough to reach the MDE; in a go-dark the treated geos need enough current spend to withhold6.

  5. Size the holdout

    Standard GeoLifts usually need more than half of locations as controls. Inverse GeoLift holds out only the treatment group, reducing holdout size and opportunity cost "without losing testing accuracy". Its main assumption is that positive and negative effects are "interchangeable": only the sign of the effect would differ between a standard and an inverse test. Check that control geos with positive synthetic weights actually receive the media, or the effect is diluted6.

  6. Pre-register

    Write down market sets, donor pool, estimator, KPI, test window, cooldown, α and sidedness before launch. SC weights can be fixed before any post-period data exists12.

Why power is the hard part

Across 25 large ad experiments (about $2.8M of spend), the median ROI confidence interval was more than 100 percentage points wide. Individual sales had a coefficient of variation around 10, and informative tests "can easily require more than 10 million person-weeks"30. Geo aggregation smooths individual noise but leaves very few units, so the signal-to-noise problem stays central12.

Fit diagnostics & inference

Trust the fit before you trust the lift.

A counterfactual that cannot track the pre-period cannot be trusted in the test period. Check the fit first, then pick an inference method that matches the estimator. More detail in Synthetic Control Inference and Diagnostics.

  • Visual fit and RMSPESynthetic vs treated series over the pre-period29.
  • Imbalance metricsGeoLift's scaled L2 runs from 0 (perfect fit) to 1 (no better than a simple average of controls)15.
  • Predictor balanceA large gap between the treated unit's predictors and the synthetic control's means interpolation bias12.
  • Backdating (in-time placebo)Pretend the test started earlier; a credible model shows about zero "effect"29.
  • Leave-one-outDrop each donor or geo in turn; the estimate should not hinge on one geo292.
  • Placebo geosRun the analysis on untreated geos and expect a null26.
  • GSC factor countCross-validate the number of factors on held-out pre-periods17.
  • DiD pre-trendsInspect event-study pre-trend coefficients, but do not pick the assumption by pre-test22.

Where the conformal default comes from

That GeoLift uses conformal inference by default comes from GeoLift reference pages and vignettes as surfaced by search (one vignette URL returned 404) and from a third-party replication. The method itself is established31; the implementation detail is secondary.

Narrow is not the same as right

In the CausalImpact geo example, the 95% interval was wider than a classical two-stage regression's (13–30% vs 19–22%), while the point estimates differed by less than 5%26. Prefer the interval whose assumptions you believe.

Pitfalls

Where geo tests go wrong.

Three structural problems come with the territory. The rest are analysis habits you can fix.

Spillover

Commuters, TV and radio signals and shipping addresses cross borders practitioner knowledge If treatment benefits donors, SC estimates are biased toward zero and become a lower bound12. Use commuting zones or drop buffer geos along treated borders2812.

DMA granularity

Coarser units reduce spillover but leave fewer units2. Commuting zones "take the commuter effect into account"28 spillover link is an inference Digital geo-targeting by IP is noisy practitioner heuristic

Small N

Just over 210 geos in the US2 means wide intervals. With fewer than about 5 donors, permutation inference is not meaningful, and many donors with few periods produce spurious fit12.

Analysis habits to avoid

  1. Reporting an underpowered test as "no effect". Simulate power before launch and report the interval, not just significance3015.
  2. TWFE on staggered rollouts. Use Callaway–Sant'Anna, Sun & Abraham or another heterogeneity-robust estimator207.
  3. Overfitting the pre-period. Many donors and few periods produce spurious fit; check with backdating and leave-one-out1229.
  4. Contaminated donors. Neighbours or geos with concurrent campaigns left in the control pool12.
  5. Short post-period with lagged effects. Include a cooldown2.
  6. Changing the design after seeing results. Pre-register12.
  7. Ignoring substitution. Paid traffic may be cannibalizing organic traffic8.
  8. Falling back on observational attribution. In one activity-bias study, regression gave about 1198% "lift" against an RCT truth of 5.4%32, and observational methods often failed to recover Facebook RCT effects33.
  9. Ignoring anticipation. If behaviour shifts before launch, for example after a pre-announcement, backdate the intervention date1229.

Check your aggregation choice

A third-party replication reported that GeoLift's conformal p-value changes when treated outcomes are summed rather than averaged (p ≈ 0.68 vs ≈ 0.01). This has not been verified against Meta's own documentation unverified Treat it as a reason to check how your tool aggregates treated geos.

Worked example

A search campaign across 190 DMAs.

The CausalImpact paper's own application is a randomized geo experiment. It shows the whole workflow: design, counterfactual, robustness checks and an honest reading of the interval. Summary from the author's note CausalImpact Empirical Application26.

  • 95 / 190DMAs randomized to the treatment groupBrodersen et al.26
  • 22%Cumulative lift, 95% interval 13% to 30%Randomized controls26
  • 2%Placebo "effect" on untreated DMAs, interval −6% to 10%Placebo test26

  1. Design

    A 6-week search campaign ran in 95 of 190 DMAs, assigned at random. About a year of pre-period data sat behind it26.

  2. Counterfactual

    The KPI was search-related visits to the advertiser's site (organic plus paid clicks). For this application the model used a local level plus a static regression on the control series, with a spike-and-slab prior choosing which controls to use. The post-period counterfactual was drawn from the posterior26. The general CausalImpact model can also add trend and seasonality components2425.

  3. Result

    With the randomized control DMAs as controls, the cumulative lift was about 88,400 additional clicks, or 22% [13%, 30%]. The effect peaked about 3 weeks into the campaign and faded about a week after it ended26.

  4. Robustness

    Swapping the randomized controls for purely observational ones (Google Trends search volume for the advertiser's industry) gave 21% [12%, 30%]. Running the same analysis on untreated DMAs gave a placebo of 2% [−6%, 10%], an interval that comfortably covers zero26.

  5. Reading the interval

    A classical two-stage regression (Vaver & Koehler's approach) produced a much narrower interval (19–22%), and CausalImpact's point estimate differed from it by less than 5%26. The author's note reads the wider interval as an honest account of posterior uncertainty: narrower is not automatically better.

One case study

The close agreement between randomized and observational controls is a single case. It does not show that observational controls will work in general; randomize the geos when you can.
